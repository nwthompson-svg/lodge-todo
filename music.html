STEP 1

Who it's for

The parent pays. The teenager decides. You have to win both.

"Anyone who wants lessons" is nobody. Your best clients look like this:

  • The kid: 11 to 17, wants to be in a band, and is bored stiff by grade books and scales. They want to play the songs they actually listen to.
  • The parent: wants them off their phone, more confident, with a group of mates and something good for their CV or university application. They're time-poor, so you coming to their house is worth real money to them.
  • Where: within about 10 miles of you in Castle Cary, and the ring of money round Bruton. Independent-school families (King's Bruton, Hazlegrove, Millfield, Downside, Sherborne, All Hallows, Port Regis), London families with weekend and second homes, and the farm and estate families. Plus Frome, Castle Cary and Wells, which have plenty of busy parents too.

The daytime problem

Kids are only free from about 15:30 to 19:30 on weekdays, and on Saturdays. That's only about 25 hours a week you can sell to kids. Daytime has to come from somewhere else:

  • Adults who always wanted to play: retired people, people who work from home, the dad who wants to play at a mate's wedding.
  • Schools that pay visiting teachers to teach in the school day (step 6).
  • Home-schooled kids.
Your ideal-client testLives within 20 minutes. Comes every week. Pays a term up front. Would tell their friends. If a family ticks all four, that's who you aim every word at.

STEP 2

What you're selling

A proposition is one sentence that tells the right parent: this is for my kid, and nobody else does it.

Here's what you've got that a normal music teacher hasn't:

  • You actually gig. Thirty years of it, from the Four Seasons in Geneva to Foo Fakers and Nirvanot at Teddy Rocks and the Cheese & Grain. You're not a teacher who used to play.
  • Guitar and voice. You can make a kid into the one who plays and sings at the front.
  • Down to earth. Teenagers will listen to you when they won't listen to a teacher.
  • You can put bands together and get them a real gig.
  • Nobody does this locally. I couldn't find any band coaching for teenagers in Bruton, Frome, Wells or Shepton Mallet. The nearest is The Rock Project in Bath and Bristol.

What you charge now

The ceiling

For one-to-one at home, the ceiling is about what the top schools charge, £60 an hour, plus whatever coming to them is worth. Some families will pay £70 or more. Find that out by trying it on new families, not by guessing. The bigger money isn't in pushing the hourly rate. It's in groups (step 4).

Three price rules
  1. Start at the price you mean to keep. Raising prices on families you already have is much harder than starting high.
  2. Sell by the term, paid up front. 10 to 12 weeks. Never pay-as-you-go. That one rule decides whether this works (step 9).
  3. If everyone says yes straight away, you're too cheap. About one in five saying "that's a bit much" is about right.

STEP 4

The numbers

Count weeks, not hours. School terms give you about 36 teaching weeks a year, not 52.

Say you want about £40,000 a year coming in. Take off about £6,800 of running costs and about £5,700 of tax and NI (both worked out below), and that's roughly £28,500 in your pocket, a decent site wage. From lessons alone, over 36 weeks, £40,000 is £1,111 a week.

About the mileage. Because you work from home, the drive to every lesson counts as business mileage. HMRC lets you claim 55p a mile for the first 10,000 miles a year (it went up from 45p in April 2026). Only about £750 to £800 of that is fuel you'll actually buy, at about £1.40 a litre. The rest pays for the wear on your vehicle: tyres, servicing, and replacing it one day. All of it comes off your tax bill.

Once you use the flat rate for a vehicle, you have to stick with it for that vehicle. And you can't use it on a vehicle you've already claimed capital allowances on, for example for the building work. Ask an accountant if the van's already in your building accounts.

STEP 5

Brand and website

The brand is you. A gigging musician the kids think is cool and the parents trust.

The name

You've got it: Future Rock. Keep it, spell it one way everywhere (website, Instagram, Facebook, Google, the van), and always put a line under it that says what and where: "Future Rock: guitar, vocals and bands, Castle Cary, Bruton and Frome".

The look

  • Photos of you playing live, not a stock photo of a guitar.
  • Video of your pupils playing a gig as soon as you have one, with the parents' written OK first (step 9). Thirty seconds of a 14-year-old band nailing a song does more selling than any words.
  • Plain and simple. Two colours, one font, the same photo of you everywhere.

The website: one page is enough

  1. The top line: your proposition (step 2), with a photo of you on stage.
  2. Who it's for: age, area, "no grade books unless you want them".
  3. What you get: lessons, the band, the gig, camps.
  4. Prices. Show them, or at least "from £45". It puts off the time-wasters, and almost nobody round here does it.
  5. About you: your bands, the gigs, why you teach.
  6. What parents say, once you have it.
  7. "DBS checked. Fully insured." Parents look for it.
  8. The area you cover: Bruton, Castle Cary, Frome, Wincanton, Shepton, Wells.
  9. One big button: "WhatsApp me", plus a short form.

You're already on GoDaddy's website builder, so there's no need to move. Change the words and the order, and keep everything else. AI can write every word of it with you (step 14).

STEP 6

How people find you

Cheapest and best first. Don't spend a penny on ads until the free stuff is done.

1. The first five families: people you know

Parents at your kids' school, the band's followers, Neil's lot, the pub. Tell everyone, every time. Your first five are what give you reviews, photos and the first band.

2. Google Business Profile (free, and the most important thing)

This is the box with the map that comes up when a parent searches "guitar lessons near me". Set one up (free, at google.com/business), put in your area, photos and prices, and ask every happy family for a Google review. Ten good reviews and you'll beat most teachers round here.

3. Schools (this fills the daytime)

Schools hire visiting music teachers as self-employed staff. Email or ring the Head of Music directly, because many of these jobs never get advertised. They'll want an enhanced DBS check, references, and often a trial lesson.

Worth knowing: Sexey's, in Bruton itself, published its music lessons list for 2024/25 and there was no guitar on it. Its drum and singing teachers are self-employed and parents pay them directly, £20 for 30 minutes. That's the nearest open door there is. Ask whether it's still open.

4. The gig is the advert

Every end-of-term gig puts 30 to 60 parents in a room. Every one of them films it and sends it to their friends. Venues to try: the pubs, village halls, and Frome's Cheese & Grain, which already runs free Sunday sessions for young musicians and could be a partner.

5. Local Facebook groups, village magazines and noticeboards

Free, or nearly. Post the gig videos rather than "lessons available".

6. Pay-per-click: do you need it?

Not yet, and you don't need to be an expert. Here's all of it:

  • Google ads catch people who are already looking ("guitar lessons Frome"). You pay Google each time someone clicks your ad, not when it's shown. For local services in the UK that's usually £1.50 to £3 a click. I couldn't find a figure for music lessons; Google tells you the real one when you set it up.
  • Facebook and Instagram ads reach people who weren't looking: parents in BA10, BA11 and BA7 who see your kid-band video and think "oh, that's for Ollie". For this business that's often better than Google.
  • Rough sum: £5 a day is £150 a month and maybe 50 to 100 clicks. If a handful turn into enquiries and one or two sign up, that's £150 to win a family worth £1,600 a year. Good business, but only once the website and Google profile are right. Otherwise you're paying to send people to a shop with nothing in the window.
  • Keep it tight: 15 miles round you, your town names in the words. Turn it off in August, when nobody's booking.
Track itAsk every enquiry: "How did you hear about me?" Write it down. After three months you'll know what's working, and you can stop paying for what isn't.

STEP 7

How they get hold of you

One number, WhatsApp, and answer fast. The teacher who replies first usually gets the pupil.
  • One way in: a WhatsApp Business number (free app) on the website, Google and every post. Set it up to send a polite automatic reply when you're teaching.
  • Reply the same day. If you can, reply within the hour. Rich, busy parents ask two or three teachers at once.
  • Message the parents, never the kids. Always (step 9).

The first phone call

  1. Ask about the kid, not the lessons. How old? What do they listen to? Do they play already? Do they want to be in a band?
  2. Ask the parent what they want. Confidence, mates, getting off screens, grades? Whatever they say is what you sell back to them.
  3. Paint the picture: "By Easter I'd want them playing two songs they love, and if they're up for it, in a band for the summer gig."
  4. Book a first lesson there and then. A trial lesson at the normal price, or free if you'd rather. Put it in the diary before you hang up.
  5. After the trial: send the term booking, the price, and your terms in one message. Booked and paid within the week.

STEP 8

Defending your price

Say the price once, clearly, then stop talking. Whoever fills the silence loses.

When they say "that's more than the school"

"It is a bit. I come to you, so there's no running about. It's the music they actually want to play. And they get a band and a real gig every term. Most parents tell me that's what keeps them at it."

When they say "I found someone for £25"

"There are definitely cheaper lessons out there. If it's just learning chords, that might suit you better. What I do is get them playing in a band, on a stage. If that's what they want, I'm your man."

Never cut the price of an hour. If money's tight for a family, offer a different thing instead: a 30-minute lesson, a shared lesson with a mate, or band sessions only. The price of your hour stays the same.

Put prices up a little every September, with a term's notice, by letter. Families expect it. Do it once a year, never in the middle of a year.

Practise saying it. This is the bit most people find hardest, and you can rehearse it with AI as many times as you like (step 14).

Safeguarding: the one that ends it overnight

You'll be alone with other people's children. One allegation, even a false one, can finish the business. Protect yourself from day one:

  • Enhanced DBS check. Since January 2026, self-employed people can apply for one themselves, through an umbrella body. The law doesn't make you have one for private lessons, but every school and most parents will expect it. It costs about £73 through an umbrella body. Then join the DBS Update Service (£15 a year) so one certificate does for everyone.
  • Join the Musicians' Union (about £24 a month, £287 a year). It includes £10 million public liability insurance that covers teaching, plus help with DBS and contracts. Check the current terms with them when you join.
  • A short safeguarding course online, and a one-page safeguarding policy on your website. Schools ask for both.
  • House rules: a parent in the house for every lesson, a door left open, messages to parents only, never to kids on their own phones or socials.
  • Written consent from parents before any photo or video of their child goes anywhere.

STEP 10

Growing it into something bigger

A job pays you while you work. A business is worth something even when you're not there. The aim is to move from the first to the second.
  1. 1Your diary (year 1)You, teaching. Worth exactly what you earn. Get the price, the terms and the reputation right.
  2. 2The band programme (years 1–2)Bands, termly gigs, camps. More money per hour, and the gigs bring the next families in.
  3. 3Other teachers under your brand (years 2–3)Local musicians on drums, bass and keys, working for themselves under your name. Say a parent pays £45: the teacher gets about £32, and you keep about £13 for finding the family, booking and billing. Ten teachers doing 20 lessons a week each is about £2,600 a week across your bands, while you teach less.
  4. 4A brand with value (years 3–5)More towns (Frome, Wells, Sherborne), school contracts, holiday camps, recording days, a regular young-bands gig night. Now there's a client list, termly income that comes back every term, a team and a name. That can be sold, franchised, or run by you from the side as you get older.

The model's been proven. Rocksteady (in-school bands, about £45 a month per kid) and The Rock Project (bands and camps in Bath and Bristol) are both businesses built on exactly this. Nobody's doing it round here yet.

Once other teachers are billing through Future Rock, your takings can pass the £90,000 VAT threshold. Get an accountant's view before you get there, because how the teachers are set up changes the answer.

What makes it worth something
  • Termly income that comes back every term without you chasing it.
  • Systems written down: booking, terms, safeguarding, the camp plan.
  • A name parents recognise.
  • It doesn't fall over if you're off for a fortnight.

Does it make financial sense?

Yes, as long as it fills hours the premium work can't. Parents only want you after school and on Saturdays. Schools, SEND and care homes want you in the daytime, and holiday clubs want you in the holidays. So it adds to your week instead of competing with it.

One day a week of this (a school or SEND day at £295, plus two care-home sessions at £60) comes to about £15,000 a year, all from hours you'd otherwise have empty.

Where it doesn't make sense: pricing below the MU minimum because it's "for a good cause", or spending unpaid days writing bids for small pots. And a holiday-club day is thinner than it looks: £400 for 16 kids, less about £64 of food and £112 of hall, leaves about £220. Mix paying places in alongside the funded ones and it's a different story.

The bridge: the rich families pay it forward

A "Future Rock bursary": a little of every premium family's fee, plus a grant, pays for free places for kids who couldn't afford it. Wealthy parents like knowing their money does some good. Funders like a business that puts its own money in. And it's a story the local press will print.

It's also worth a conversation with the Octagon Music Hub, which has run music education for Somerset and Dorset since 2024. It pays partners to deliver SEND and inclusion work. This year's round is closed, but it invites people to book a meeting ahead of the next one.

STEP 12

Money that isn't your hours

An hourly rate has a ceiling, and the ceiling is you. Over ten years, the money that lasts comes from groups, from things that repeat every term, and from other people delivering your programme.

A full diary of one-to-one lessons tops out at about £30,000 take-home (step 4). It stops the day you stop. Three things change that:

  1. More people per hour: bands, camps, school groups.
  2. Other people's hours: teachers and tutors delivering your programme while the business keeps a margin.
  3. Things that sell without you in the room: a name, a written-down method, events, a licence.

Expect lots of questions

Future Rock in Schools: the daytime business

Bands of five or six kids, 30 minutes a week in the school day, and their parents pay a monthly subscription. It's the same idea as your evenings, sold in term time, to a different set of parents.

This is where the whole idea came from: your Rocksteady interview. This tab works out how their model makes money, who else does it, what's left locally, and what it could make you.

Same business or a separate one?

The same business, Future Rock, with two halves: "In schools" in the daytime and "Lessons & bands" in the evenings.
  • One name, one reputation. A parent who sees their 8-year-old play the school concert is already a Future Rock parent when that child turns 11.
  • The schools are where your teenagers come from. Rocksteady stops at Year 6. Future Rock doesn't, so the primary bands feed straight into your teen lessons and bands.
  • The paperwork's shared: the same DBS, insurance, safeguarding policy, website and bank.
  • They fit together in the week. Schools take 9 till 3, and lessons take 3.30 till 7.30 and Saturdays. Between them they fill the week that evening lessons alone leave empty.

If it grows to several teachers, it might later become its own limited company for the employment side. That's a decision for when it happens, with an accountant.

SCHOOLS 1

How Rocksteady's model works

The school pays nothing and lends a room. The parents pay a monthly direct debit, and a band leader runs a string of small bands one after another through the school day.
  • The lesson: a band of children (iRock caps it at 9) plays together for 30 minutes a week in the school day. Drums, keys, guitar, bass and vocals, with the instruments provided. Ages 4 to 11, Reception to Year 6.
  • The price round here: £46.48 a month at every local Rocksteady school (checked on their booking system, 21/10/2026). iRock is £45.99 at Wincanton Primary.
  • The school: pays nothing, provides a room, sends the letter home and lets them do a taster assembly. The company does all the admin with parents.
  • The concerts: the bands play to the school and the parents through the year. That's the bit parents film and share, and it's what sells the next term.
  • The free places: Rocksteady says it gave over £3 million of free teaching last year, "so no child is left behind". iRock gives one bursary place per school. Schools like it, and it makes the whole thing easy for a head teacher to say yes to.

A band leader's week

Up to 10 schools a week, usually two a day, Monday to Friday in the daytime only. You load and unload the kit from your own car at every school, then run one 30-minute band after another, with a concert each term. Then 11-plus weeks off with the school holidays.

SCHOOLS 7

The rules

  • Charging parents in school hours is allowed for this. Schools can't normally charge for anything in the school day. Music is the exception: group or individual instrument tuition can be charged when a parent asks for it (Charges for Music Tuition (England) Regulations 2007). Children in care can't be charged, and the school needs a charging and remissions policy.
  • Safeguarding is the big one. Teaching children every week is "regulated activity". You need an enhanced DBS check with the children's barred list (the self-employed route through an umbrella body, as in the other tab). The school puts you on its single central record, checks your ID every visit, and wants your safeguarding policy. That's Keeping Children Safe in Education 2026, in force since 01/09/2026.
  • Some schools ask visiting teachers for a room fee. It's not common in primaries, but ask up front.
  • Write it down. A short agreement with each school: the room, the times, the free place, safeguarding, and who tells parents what.

Start with one school, Castle Cary, where you live. Make it brilliant, film the concert, then take that film to the next head teacher.
  1. Fix the gap Rocksteady found. Get hours with young children before you pitch a school: holiday clubs (step 11 in the other tab), school workshop days, helping at your boys' school, a Cubs or Brownies session. Get a head teacher to give you a reference.
  2. Build the method. A term of songs, a simple level system kids can climb, a concert at the end, and a short report to parents each term. Rocksteady and iRock both grade the children's progress, and parents love it.
  3. Kit: one portable rig you can set up in 10 minutes. Second-hand, or grant-funded (step 13).
  4. The pitch to the head: free to the school, a free place for a child who needs it, a concert for the school, fully DBS-checked, all admin done for them. Offer a free taster assembly.
  5. The letter home: the school sends it. Parents sign up online and pay by direct debit. Start when you have 2 bands.
  6. Then the villages: Ditcheat, North Cadbury, Evercreech, Keinton, Queen Camel. Group them so one day covers two schools that are close together.
  7. Hire when you're full. When you're turning schools away, your first band leader is the musician you'd have been in that interview.
Another option worth thinking aboutReapply to Rocksteady in a year with that experience with children. It's a salaried, term-time job, and your evenings and Saturdays stay free for Future Rock. Read the contract carefully for anything that stops you teaching privately or working with "their" schools.
