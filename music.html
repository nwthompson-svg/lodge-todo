Wayne, Neil told me about your chat. You want to go from site work to teaching music full time over the next six months. This page is the whole road, from your first paying pupil to a business that's worth something.

Read it once, top to bottom. Then come back to whichever step you're on. There are two things on here to play with: a money calculator and a proposition builder. They save what you type on your phone.

The whole thing in one line

You're not selling guitar lessons. You're selling the moment a kid walks on stage with their own band, and their parents film it.

Lessons are just how you get there. Parents pay for the stage moment, and they pay a lot more for it than for half an hour with a grade book.

STEP 1

Who it's for

The parent pays. The teenager decides. You have to win both.

"Anyone who wants lessons" is nobody. Your best clients look like this:

  • The kid: 11 to 17, wants to be in a band, and is bored stiff by grade books and scales. They want to play the songs they actually listen to.
  • The parent: wants them off their phone, more confident, with a group of mates and something good for their CV or university application. They're time-poor, so you coming to their house is worth real money to them.
  • Where: the ring of money round Bruton. Independent-school families (King's Bruton, Hazlegrove, Millfield, Downside, Sherborne, All Hallows, Port Regis), London families with weekend and second homes, and the farm and estate families. Plus Frome, Castle Cary and Wells, which have plenty of busy parents too.

The daytime problem

Kids are only free from about 15:30 to 19:30 on weekdays, and on Saturdays. That's only about 25 hours a week you can sell to kids. Daytime has to come from somewhere else:

  • Adults who always wanted to play: retired people, people who work from home, the dad who wants to play at a mate's wedding.
  • Schools that pay visiting teachers to teach in the school day (step 6).
  • Home-schooled kids.
Your ideal-client testLives within 20 minutes. Comes every week. Pays a term up front. Would tell their friends. If a family ticks all four, that's who you aim every word at.

STEP 2

What you're selling

A proposition is one sentence that tells the right parent: this is for my kid, and nobody else does it.

Here's what you've got that a normal music teacher hasn't:

  • You actually gig. You're in a working band, not just a teacher who used to play.
  • Guitar and voice. You can make a kid into the one who plays and sings at the front.
  • Down to earth. Teenagers will listen to you when they won't listen to a teacher.
  • You can put bands together and get them a real gig.
  • Nobody does this locally. I couldn't find any band coaching for teenagers in Bruton, Frome, Wells or Shepton Mallet. The nearest is The Rock Project in Bath and Bristol.

Make it a ladder, not just lessons

  1. 1One-to-one lessonsGuitar or singing, at their house, songs they love
  2. 2The band4 or 5 of your pupils put together, rehearsing weekly or fortnightly
  3. 3The gigAn end-of-term show in a pub, village hall or venue. Parents fill the room
  4. 4Holiday band campA week in Easter or summer: form a band Monday, gig Friday
  5. 5ExtrasRecording day, rock and pop grades for the ones who want them

Grades aren't the enemy. Trinity Rock & Pop and RSL Rockschool grades are rock and pop exams. At grades 6 to 8 they carry UCAS points, the same as classical grades. A parent who's worried "it's just mucking about in a band" hears that and relaxes. Offer grades as an option, never as the point.

Build yours

Fill these in. The sentence builds itself underneath. It saves on your phone.

Test itRead it out to three parents you know. If they say "oh, my lad would love that", you've got it. If they say "so, guitar lessons?", it isn't sharp enough yet.

The price

Your price isn't set by other guitar teachers. It's set by what the parent would otherwise pay, and by what you're saving them.

The ceiling

For one-to-one at home, the ceiling is about what the top schools charge, £60 an hour, plus whatever coming to them is worth. Some families will pay £70 or more. Find that out by trying it on new families, not by guessing. The bigger money isn't in pushing the hourly rate. It's in groups (step 4).

Three price rules
  1. Start at the price you mean to keep. Raising prices on families you already have is much harder than starting high.
  2. Sell by the term, paid up front. 10 to 12 weeks. Never pay-as-you-go. That one rule decides whether this works (step 9).
  3. If everyone says yes straight away, you're too cheap. About one in five saying "that's a bit much" is about right.

STEP 4

The numbers

Count weeks, not hours. School terms give you about 36 teaching weeks a year, not 52.

Say you want about £40,000 a year coming in. Take off about £6,000 of costs (below) and that's roughly a decent site wage before tax. From lessons alone, over 36 weeks, that's £1,111 a week.

£4028: only just
£4525: a full diary
£5520: room to breathe

This is the most important table on the page. About 26 home-visit lessons a week is the most you can fit: 4 a night, Monday to Friday, with driving, plus about 6 on a Saturday. At £30 it can't be done, however hard you work. Price isn't greed. It's whether the business works at all.

Why groups change everything

One band session: 5 kids at £22 = £110 for 90 minutes, about £73 an hour. A camp week: 8 kids, 5 days, £60 = £2,400. Add one band session a week and two camp weeks a year, and you only need about 19 lessons a week instead of 25.

How many families

A full diary is about 25 to 30 families. Some leave every year: exams, moving house, losing interest. Plan on a quarter to a third going, which means 8 to 10 new families a year just to stand still. That's what your marketing is for.

One family is worth about £1,600 a year (£45 × 36 weeks), and maybe £3,000 to £5,000 over the two or three years they stay. Keep that number in your head. It tells you what you can afford to spend to win one.

STEP 5

Brand and website

The brand is you. A gigging musician the kids think is cool and the parents trust.

The name

Pick one and stick to it everywhere: website, Instagram, Facebook, Google, the van. The name and the line underneath should say what and where, for example "[Your name] Music: guitar, singing and bands, Bruton and Frome". If the name you and Ruth started with does that, keep it.

The look

  • Photos of you playing live, not a stock photo of a guitar.
  • Video of your pupils playing a gig as soon as you have one, with the parents' written OK first (step 9). Thirty seconds of a 14-year-old band nailing a song does more selling than any words.
  • Plain and simple. Two colours, one font, the same photo of you everywhere.

The website: one page is enough

  1. The top line: your proposition (step 2), with a photo of you on stage.
  2. Who it's for: age, area, "no grade books unless you want them".
  3. What you get: lessons, the band, the gig, camps.
  4. Prices. Show them, or at least "from £45". It puts off the time-wasters, and almost nobody round here does it.
  5. About you: your bands, the gigs, why you teach.
  6. What parents say, once you have it.
  7. "DBS checked. Fully insured." Parents look for it.
  8. The area you cover: Bruton, Castle Cary, Frome, Wincanton, Shepton, Wells.
  9. One big button: "WhatsApp me", plus a short form.

Squarespace or Wix do this for about £15 to £25 a month. You can run it yourself from your phone. AI can write every word of it with you (step 11).

STEP 6

How people find you

Cheapest and best first. Don't spend a penny on ads until the free stuff is done.

1. The first five families: people you know

Parents at your kids' school, the band's followers, Neil's lot, the pub. Tell everyone, every time. Your first five are what give you reviews, photos and the first band.

2. Google Business Profile (free, and the most important thing)

This is the box with the map that comes up when a parent searches "guitar lessons near me". Set one up (free, at google.com/business), put in your area, photos and prices, and ask every happy family for a Google review. Ten good reviews and you'll beat most teachers round here.

3. Schools (this fills the daytime)

Schools hire visiting music teachers as self-employed staff. Email or ring the Head of Music directly, because many of these jobs never get advertised. They'll want an enhanced DBS check, references, and often a trial lesson.

Worth knowing: Sexey's, in Bruton itself, published its music lessons list for 2024/25 and there was no guitar on it. Its drum and singing teachers are self-employed and parents pay them directly, £20 for 30 minutes. That's the nearest open door there is. Ask whether it's still open.

4. The gig is the advert

Every end-of-term gig puts 30 to 60 parents in a room. Every one of them films it and sends it to their friends. Venues to try: the pubs, village halls, and Frome's Cheese & Grain, which already runs free Sunday sessions for young musicians and could be a partner.

5. Local Facebook groups, village magazines and noticeboards

Free, or nearly. Post the gig videos rather than "lessons available".

6. Pay-per-click: do you need it?

Not yet, and you don't need to be an expert. Here's all of it:

  • Google ads catch people who are already looking ("guitar lessons Frome"). You pay Google each time someone clicks your ad, not when it's shown. For local services in the UK that's usually £1.50 to £3 a click. I couldn't find a figure for music lessons; Google tells you the real one when you set it up.
  • Facebook and Instagram ads reach people who weren't looking: parents in BA10, BA11 and BA7 who see your kid-band video and think "oh, that's for Ollie". For this business that's often better than Google.
  • Rough sum: £5 a day is £150 a month and maybe 50 to 100 clicks. If a handful turn into enquiries and one or two sign up, that's £150 to win a family worth £1,600 a year. Good business, but only once the website and Google profile are right. Otherwise you're paying to send people to a shop with nothing in the window.
  • Keep it tight: 15 miles round you, your town names in the words. Turn it off in August, when nobody's booking.
Track itAsk every enquiry: "How did you hear about me?" Write it down. After three months you'll know what's working, and you can stop paying for what isn't.

STEP 7

How they get hold of you

One number, WhatsApp, and answer fast. The teacher who replies first usually gets the pupil.
  • One way in: a WhatsApp Business number (free app) on the website, Google and every post. Set it up to send a polite automatic reply when you're teaching.
  • Reply the same day. If you can, reply within the hour. Rich, busy parents ask two or three teachers at once.
  • Message the parents, never the kids. Always (step 9).

The first phone call

  1. Ask about the kid, not the lessons. How old? What do they listen to? Do they play already? Do they want to be in a band?
  2. Ask the parent what they want. Confidence, mates, getting off screens, grades? Whatever they say is what you sell back to them.
  3. Paint the picture: "By Easter I'd want them playing two songs they love, and if they're up for it, in a band for the summer gig."
  4. Book a first lesson there and then. A trial lesson at the normal price, or free if you'd rather. Put it in the diary before you hang up.
  5. After the trial: send the term booking, the price, and your terms in one message. Booked and paid within the week.

STEP 8

Defending your price

Say the price once, clearly, then stop talking. Whoever fills the silence loses.

When they say "that's more than the school"

"It is a bit. I come to you, so there's no running about. It's the music they actually want to play. And they get a band and a real gig every term. Most parents tell me that's what keeps them at it."

When they say "I found someone for £25"

"There are definitely cheaper lessons out there. If it's just learning chords, that might suit you better. What I do is get them playing in a band, on a stage. If that's what they want, I'm your man."

Never cut the price of an hour. If money's tight for a family, offer a different thing instead: a 30-minute lesson, a shared lesson with a mate, or band sessions only. The price of your hour stays the same.

Put prices up a little every September, with a term's notice, by letter. Families expect it. Do it once a year, never in the middle of a year.

Practise saying it. This is the bit most people find hardest, and you can rehearse it with AI as many times as you like (step 11).

STEP 9

The things that can sink it

Most teaching businesses don't die from a lack of pupils. They die from cancellations, holidays and a cheap price.
The riskWhat protects you
PriceThe step 4 table. £10 a lesson is the difference between a full diary and one that can't pay you.
CancellationsTermly up-front payment. Missed lessons aren't refunded. One swap per term with 48 hours' notice. Put it in writing before the first lesson.
HolidaysAbout 16 weeks a year with no lessons. Fill them with camps. Pay yourself the same amount every month from a separate account, so a quiet August doesn't hurt.
The seasonsSeptember and January are when families sign up. May and June, exam kids drop off. Push your marketing just before the start of each term.
DrivingEvery minute in the van is unpaid. Group families by village and evening: Bruton Mondays, Frome Tuesdays. Maybe add a teaching room later for the kids who'll come to you.
It's all youIll or injured means no income. Look at income-protection insurance. The long-term answer is other teachers (step 10).
Evenings and SaturdaysYou'll be working when your own kids are home. Talk it through with Ruth before you start, not after. Keep one evening and Sunday fixed and off.
ReputationWealthy families talk to each other, for good and bad. On time, every time. Tidy messages. Say what you'll do, then do it.

Safeguarding: the one that ends it overnight

You'll be alone with other people's children. One allegation, even a false one, can finish the business. Protect yourself from day one:

  • Enhanced DBS check. Since January 2026, self-employed people can apply for one themselves, through an umbrella body. The law doesn't make you have one for private lessons, but every school and most parents will expect it. Join the DBS Update Service so one certificate does for everyone.
  • Join the Musicians' Union (about £23 a month). It includes £10 million public liability insurance that covers teaching, plus help with DBS and contracts. Check the current terms with them when you join.
  • A short safeguarding course online, and a one-page safeguarding policy on your website. Schools ask for both.
  • House rules: a parent in the house for every lesson, a door left open, messages to parents only, never to kids on their own phones or socials.
  • Written consent from parents before any photo or video of their child goes anywhere.

STEP 10

Growing it into something bigger

A job pays you while you work. A business is worth something even when you're not there. The aim is to move from the first to the second.
  1. 1Your diary (year 1)You, teaching. Worth exactly what you earn. Get the price, the terms and the reputation right.
  2. 2The band programme (years 1–2)Bands, termly gigs, camps. More money per hour, and the gigs bring the next families in.
  3. 3Other teachers under your brand (years 2–3)Local musicians on drums, bass and keys, working for themselves under your name. Say a parent pays £45: the teacher gets about £32, and you keep about £13 for finding the family, booking and billing. Ten teachers doing 20 lessons a week each is about £2,600 a week across your bands, while you teach less.
  4. 4A brand with value (years 3–5)More towns (Frome, Wells, Sherborne), school contracts, holiday camps, recording days, a regular young-bands gig night. Now there's a client list, termly income that comes back every term, a team and a name. That can be sold, franchised, or run by you from the side as you get older.

The model's been proven. Rocksteady (in-school bands, about £45 a month per kid) and The Rock Project (bands and camps in Bath and Bristol) are both businesses built on exactly this. Nobody's doing it round here yet.

What makes it worth something
  • Termly income that comes back every term without you chasing it.
  • Systems written down: booking, terms, safeguarding, the camp plan.
  • A name parents recognise.
  • It doesn't fall over if you're off for a fortnight.

STEP 11

Using AI as your coach

You don't need to learn marketing first. Get an AI to interview you, one question at a time, and do each step with it.

Use Claude (claude.ai) or ChatGPT on your phone. The free versions will do to start. Talking out loud with voice mode works well in the van. Neil uses this every day and can show you how he sets it up.

How to get good answers

  • Tell it who you are and where every time you start a new chat (copy the first box below).
  • Ask it to interview you rather than tell you. Its answers get far better.
  • Ask for one step at a time, and say "explain it like I'm a builder, not a marketer".
  • It doesn't know Bruton unless you tell it. It can be confidently wrong, so check anything legal, tax or insurance with a real person.

STEP 12

The first six months

Lessons are after school and on Saturdays, so the first months can run alongside site work. You switch when the diary says so, not on a date.
Nov
Get ready. Name, proposition and prices settled. Join the MU (insurance). Apply for the enhanced DBS. Set up the Google Business Profile and WhatsApp Business. Tell everyone you know, and book the first trial lessons.
Dec
Get seen. Website live. Get photos and video at one of your own gigs. Sell a "give a term of guitar lessons" Christmas voucher. Wealthy parents love a present that isn't more stuff.
Jan
First termly families. January is a signing-up month. Email the Heads of Music, Sexey's first. If the website's ready, switch on £5-a-day ads.
Feb
The first band. Put four or five pupils together. Book a venue for an end-of-term gig. Ask every family for a Google review.
Mar
The first gig. Film it (with consent). Post it everywhere. Take Easter camp bookings at the gig, while the parents are proud.
Apr
Easter camp, then the check. Run your first camp. Count your numbers against the switch point below. Book the summer term.
The switch point (suggested)Go full time when all three are true:
  1. 15 or more families paying termly, up front.
  2. Enquiries still coming in each month, without you chasing.
  3. Three months of money in the bank to get through the first summer holiday.
