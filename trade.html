The short version

  1. Stone and old-building work round here is short of skilled people. New-house building isn't; it's quiet.
  2. £30 an hour is about the top of what a sub gets for brick or stone here. The extra money is in pricing whole jobs, and that brings costs a sub doesn't carry.
  3. Read 1 lays out the ways people with your skills earn more, side by side, including staying as you are.

Read 1 · Part 1, the market. Is there really a shortage? Where's the work? Does the trade fade?

You'll hear

"There's a shortage of bricklayers"

Partly

Fewer people are coming into the trade. Bricklaying apprentice starts in England fell by more than a third in three years:

2021/223,130 starts
2024/251,910 starts

About 50 people a year start a stonemason apprenticeship in the whole of England.

But building new houses is quiet right now. Brick deliveries were down 23% on a year in July, and brick factories are laying people off. So it's a slow squeeze on skilled people, not a boom.

Most of the loudest "shortage" talk comes from people with something to gain: training bodies, housebuilders wanting visas, robot makers. That doesn't make it wrong. It's why this part checks the numbers.

You'll hear

"Bricklaying's dying out"

Not for stone

The government's own projection has bricklaying jobs falling 6% by 2035, before counting retirements. Timber frame is about 12–15% of new homes in England (it's over 90% in Scotland). Bricklaying robots exist, in trials.

Old walls, lime work and listed repairs can't be made in a factory or by a robot.

You'll hear

"The ones who set themselves up right will get the best work"

Partly, not proven

37% of stonemasonry firms always charge extra for heritage skill. But 55% of South West heritage firms charge nothing extra for it.

One way to read itEither clients round here won't pay more, or nobody's asking them to. The numbers can't tell you which. Historic England expects old-building skills to hit "a crisis point in the next 10–20 years".

What's happened since 2000

Shop prices×2.0
Average pay×2.4
Bricks×3.4

Pay kept up with the shops. It didn't keep up with houses. If you own your home, that went your way.

One way to read itSince 2000 the amount of money in the economy has grown about 3.4 times. Thirteen years of near-zero interest rates, and £895bn created by the Bank of England, pushed up the price of things people own. The Bank's own research says so, and also says most people benefited overall. Some people call it debasement. Gold tells the same story, louder.
You'll hear

"A postman used to be able to buy a house"

Holds up

In 2000 the average Somerset house cost about 4.6 years of a postman's pay. Now it's about 8.5. For an employed bricklayer it was 4.9 years then, and it's 8.5 now.

You'll hear

"£30 an hour is too low for what you do"

Not for labour-only. Partly for the rest.

For advertised labour-only brick and stone work round here, £30 an hour for 8 paid hours is at the top. Agency adverts this month run £24–£28 an hour. A Bristol brick-and-stone job pays £250 for a longer day.

Price guides put a tradesman's day to a homeowner at about £220–£350. That figure already includes the firm's van, insurance, quoting time, unpaid days and profit. As an illustration (not any real firm), a £300 day might split like this:

The tradesman's day£240
Van, tools, insurance£25
Quoting, unpaid days, chasing money£20
The firm's profit£15

Running a job, teaching a lad and looking after the client are real value. The market pays for them as a site role, or as the margin of whoever prices the job, not as a higher sub rate.

What £30 leaves

At £30 for 8 hours, 205 paid days a year, and about £7,500 of van, tools and insurance, you take home about £34,100 a year after tax and National Insurance. That's £2,840 a month, with nothing going into a pension. Part 3 lets you put in your own days.

CIS takes 20% as you go. On those numbers that's about £2,300 a year more than you'd owe, and it comes back after your tax return.

From April 2027, if your turnover (everything customers pay you) was over £30,000, HMRC wants quarterly updates through software (Making Tax Digital). These are rough sums for a sole trader in England, 2026/27. Your accountant can run your real figures.

How people make a move when money's tight

  • People who've put their price up usually did it on a new job, never on one they were already on.
  • They had the next job lined up before they said a number.
  • They built a buffer first. Three to six months of bills is the usual guide, often built from extra days.
  • They tried one job of their own alongside the day job.
  • Some applied for CIS gross payment status. You need £30,000 of construction turnover after materials, and your returns in on time. It stops the 20% being taken, but the first January after, the tax comes all at once, plus half of next year's in advance. On the Part 2 numbers that's about £11,400. People who managed it put money aside every week.
Small print for jobs straight for a homeowner
  • A written quote saves arguments.
  • If the job was agreed in the customer's home, they can cancel within 14 days, unless they've asked in writing for you to start sooner.
  • Listed walls need consent before work starts.
  • Garden walls over 1 m next to a road, or 2 m elsewhere, may need planning permission.
  • Work on a wall shared with a neighbour has its own rules (party walls).

How tradespeople word it

"My day rate from [month] is £___. Happy to keep this job at the old rate till it's done."
"For the wall, start to finish, it's £___. That includes the lime, the scaffold and clearing up. I can start on the ___."
When they mention a cheaper quote: "That's fair to check. Have a look whether theirs includes the scaffold and the lime. Here's everything that's in mine."

The Federation of Master Builders' advice (May 2026): explain what's behind the difference rather than dropping the price.

Read 2, the stove business

While putting this together I found Neil's notes from September, when he looked at a stove business himself, with you and your lad in mind for the fitting. It didn't add up for him as an employer paying crews. As your own business, with you doing the work in your own company, it's a different sum.

You've got the HETAS training, and you've already got a limited company: Neil's seen invoices from you self-employed and from the company. So this read treats it as two businesses side by side: the stone and brick you've always done, as yourself, and a stove business you trained for separately, in your company.

What homeowners pay for

From Neil's build diary and his notes: you glazed the last window on the Lodge, boarded and taped the gable, and laid and backfilled the long cable trench on the digger, "totally clean and tidy". The diary calls you "an absolute legend". His notes add: you run your own jobs, work alongside your son, turn up when you say, are good to have in a house, and keep it clean.

In surveys, the things homeowners fear most when they let a tradesman in are no-shows (62%) and poor work (55%).

Who's buying

About 1 home in 10 in England has a wood burner, and 1 detached house in 5. In the old Mendip area, 1 home in 10 heats with oil alone, three times the England rate.

In a 15-mile patch of mid-Somerset (about 100,000 homes), that's roughly 15,000 stoves. About 1,100 are fitted or replaced each year, and there are about 12,700 sweeps (estimates). 40 installs a year would be under 4% of the local work.

Stove sales jumped 40% in 2022 when energy prices spiked. Nothing's been published since 2023, and there's no sign yet of a 2026 rush.

You'll hear

"Wood burners are being banned"

Not for fitting or using them

New stoves must meet the Ecodesign standard. Every install is notified to building control. Your HETAS ticket lets you do that yourself, but only while your registration is current. A carbon monoxide alarm goes in with it.

Defra's 2026 plan: cleaner new stoves, coming in 3–5 years after the law is made. It doesn't propose banning fitting or using them in the countryside, and the government hasn't answered yet. New houses get heat pumps from 2027; stoves are still allowed as extra heat.

A slow squeeze on what can be sold, not on fitting.

The year in two halves

JFMAMJJASOND
Stoves (the company)   Stone (as yourself)

Stoves have a rush from September to Christmas and are quieter in summer. Stone is spring and summer. Stove work can also fill winter days that stone work loses to frost and rain. Stone stays the main trade, with fewer days of it in the stove months.

Where the jobs come from

Seven in ten homeowners hire someone they know or were recommended. For a fitter, marketing isn't standing in a kitchen selling. It's four jobs, mostly done once:

  1. A free Google profile with your phone number and area.
  2. A photo of every finished hearth.
  3. On handover day: "If you're happy with it, would you mind leaving a review? I'll text you the link."
  4. Getting on the lists of people who already meet stove buyers: showrooms that sell without fitting, online stove sellers, sweeps, builders' merchants.

Those lists bring steady fit-only work at about today's day rate. The better-paid jobs come from your own name. Lead websites charge £5–£45 a lead, shared with other trades.

One way to read itThe fitters who are busy all year aren't necessarily better at the job. They're easier to find.

A friend in the trade may be able to help with prices on stoves.

Opening up a fireplace pays about a stone day on its own. Its value is that it wins you the whole job, the kind a plain fitter has to turn down.

Pricing the fire, not your hours

"Why should I make money on someone else's stove?" The customer pays a margin on the stove whoever supplies it. The only question is whether it goes to a shop, or to the person who has to come back and put it right if it goes wrong. The law puts that on whoever supplied it.

And what's the job worth to them? A fire going before Christmas, fitted by someone who turns up, keeps the house clean and does it right first time.

Plenty of tradesmen hate saying a big number out loud. The usual way round it is paper: a printed price list ("A stove fitted with a stone hearth, from £___") and a written quote, so the page says the price.

Every £100 on the price of a supply-and-fit job is £100 of profit; at 20 jobs a year, £2,000. FMB advice: when a customer mentions a cheaper quote, explain what's in yours rather than dropping it.

VAT, plainly

Wood-burning stoves aren't zero-rated. A VAT-registered fitter has to add 20% to a homeowner's bill. A company whose turnover (everything customers pay it) stays under £90,000 in any 12 months doesn't, so it can charge less, or keep more.

Pass £90,000 in any rolling 12 months and you must register within 30 days. Your stone work as yourself and the stove company are two businesses, each with its own limit, as long as they're really separate: their own bank accounts, invoices from the right one, their own customers.

HMRC can treat a split that's only on paper as one business. Your accountant can check how yours is set up.

How businesses like this usually start

A first winterHETAS registration current, insurance checked, a free Google profile, a photo of every job, and a deposit when a stove is ordered so the customer's money buys it. A handful of installs alongside the stone work.
Years 1–2Stoves in winter, stone in summer. Every install joins the round of sweeps and services.
Years 3–5The round grows, and maybe a second pair of hands. Somewhere near £90,000 of turnover comes a choice: register for VAT and grow, or hold the volume and lift the price.

Rough running costs: public liability and tools insurance a few hundred pounds a year; HETAS registration about £450 a year (an estimate; the fee isn't published); a Google profile is free, a simple website a few hundred pounds.

Running both

  • People who run two trades usually keep a fixed share of days for their main firm, and tell them early.
  • Your lad's arrangements with anyone else he works for are his own. Other firms' customers and prices stay theirs.
  • Scaffold or a tower, when a chimney needs it, is £400–£1,500. It goes in the price.
  • If the job was agreed at a survey in the customer's home, they can cancel within 14 days. Get it in writing if they want you to start sooner.
  • If you supply the stove, putting faults right is on you (Consumer Rights Act 2015).

What the company is really for: a separate business with its own VAT limit; something you can sell or hand on (a company sells as shares, so the name, phone number and customers go with it); keeping money in for lean months; paying into a pension.

What makes it worth something

Buyers pay for what keeps going without you: a round of sweep and service customers, a second fitter who can run jobs, clean accounts, and a name and reviews the business owns.

Stove businesses for sale ask roughly half to one-and-a-bit times a year's profit. Owner-run ones with a good service round ask up to about three times. It takes three to ten years.

If your lad might take it on one day, an accountant can show you how to give him a share early, while it's worth little.

The risks, straight

  • Work swings with energy prices.
  • Online packages cap what plain jobs fetch.
  • You sign off the safety. Devon & Somerset had the most chimney fires in England in 2024/25.
  • The rules slowly tighten.
  • Someone has to answer the phone and go and look.

Three questions only you can answer

  • Which showrooms, sellers and sweeps round here would put my name on their list?
  • What would I charge for a fire with a stone hearth, if I priced the fire and not my hours?
  • Who would keep the diary and the books: someone at home, a bookkeeper, or an app?
